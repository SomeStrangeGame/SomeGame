# Lullaby audio quality checks

## Tonal hum

Hum is not limited to sub-bass. A stable oscillator in the 200–500 Hz range can
feel like pressure, especially when notes overlap. Difference tones and beating
between simultaneous fundamentals can create slower perceived pulsation.

Prefer naturally decaying recordings. Remove unnecessary bass voices, shorten
resonant tails, and avoid normalizing a quiet background until it competes with
the melody. A high-pass filter can remove rumble but cannot make an artificial
sustained timbre natural.

## Harshness

Inspect the attack before reducing the entire track volume. Plucked-string noise,
mallet clicks, upper partials, and aggressive compression are typical causes.
Use a softer source articulation, a short raised-cosine fade-in, or a gentle
low-pass filter. Do not erase the attack completely: an overly slow onset turns
each note back into a sustained electronic tone.

## Audible gaps

Distinguish musical breathing from dead air. For a disconnected phrase, bring
onsets closer or let natural tails overlap by roughly 50–200 ms. A second quiet
melodic answer can bridge space without forming a pad. Broadband noise is a poor
default gap filler.

## Natural backgrounds

A background should remain identifiable at low level. For waves, use discrete
swells with clear rise and fall instead of flat surf noise. Crossfade the chosen
source into a cyclic segment before mixing. Filter only enough to remove rumble
or hiss; excessive filtering turns water into undifferentiated noise.

## Loop seam

Silence at both endpoints is safe but may create a rhythmic pause. A musical
loop is better when the final cadence leads naturally into the opening note.
For ambience, construct a cyclic crossfade: keep the middle of a longer segment,
then append a crossfade from its tail into its head. Inspect the seam numerically,
but always validate it by listening to repeated playback.
